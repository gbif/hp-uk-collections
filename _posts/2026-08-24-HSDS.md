Would you like to explore the fossil mammals that helped shape Charles Darwin's theory of evolution, alongside stunning botanical artworks, historic archives and centuries of scientific collections? The Heritage Science Data Service (HSDS) will help make these connections possible. 

## Uncovering the Connections That Bring Collections to Life 
While DiSSCo UK is our 10-year national programme to digitise and connect specimens from UK Natural Science collections. The [Heritage Science Data Catalogue](https://hsds.ac.uk/data-catalogue/) brings together collections, archives and research data that reveal connections between people and the natural world.  

Funded by the Arts and Humanities Research Council and delivered by a consortium of partners led by the Archaeology Data Service, the HSDS provides the tools, services and expertise needed to support heritage science and conservation research.  

Through its catalogue, repository, research tools and specialist services, it helps researchers, conservators and heritage professionals preserve, manage and better understand the UK's heritage. 

## Building a National Heritage Science Resource 
Among the first resources available are from the Natural History Museum and include historically significant artworks, Charles Darwin’s fossil mammal collection, archaeological reference material and long-term environmental records. 

Darwin’s Fossil Mammals, from South America, were a key factor in his acceptance of evolution, a theory that revolutionised scientific understanding of the natural world and still informs our understanding of how life on Earth changed over time. While access to these physical collections are restricted due to their fragility, they have been scanned with 3D laser scanners to provide a digitised version of the specimens for researchers and the public to explore.  

Anna Atkins' Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions (1843) is [widely regarded as the first book illustrated with photographic images](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/anna-atkins-cyanotypes-the-first-book-of-photographs.html). Using light-sensitive chemicals and botanical specimens, Atkins created striking cyanotypes that demonstrate how collections have been recorded, studied and communicated since the nineteenth century.  

Her innovative techniques were groundbreaking, and her work continues to inspire scientists, photographers and more. This book has been digitised and is also openly available online. 

Together, these resources reveal far more than the history of individual specimens. They tell stories about how people have collected, studied and understood the natural world, and how our relationships with plants, animals and environments have changed over time.  

## Explore the collection  
These resources can now be explored through the [Museum's Heritage Science collection](https://data.nhm.ac.uk/group/heritage-data) and the [Heritage Science Data Catalogue](https://hsds.ac.uk/data-catalogue/?q=&publisher=Natural%20History%20Museum), allowing researchers and the public to discover the wider stories and contexts behind specimens. 

The catalogue will also connect users with materials from the Museum Library and Archives, including illustrations, photographs and manuscripts that help bring collections to life. It offers new ways to understand heritage, the history of collecting and humanity's changing relationship with the natural world.
